Mastering the Pickleball Third Shot Drop: Footwork, Paddle Angle & Soft Hands

By Michael Mercier • USPTA Master Professional & Former 20-Year Head Tennis Pro at Harvard University

Ask any experienced tournament player or teaching pro what separates a 3.0 recreational pickleball player from a 4.5+ championship contender, and they will give you the exact same answer: the ability to execute a reliable Third Shot Drop under pressure.

In doubles pickleball, the serving team begins at a massive structural disadvantage. The returning team is already positioned firmly at the Non-Volley Zone (NVZ or "kitchen") line, while both servers are held back at the baseline by the two-bounce rule. If the serving team hits a hard baseline drive on the third shot, the opponents simply volley the ball down at your feet, forcing errors or weak pop-ups.

The Third Shot Drop is the great equalizer. It is an unattackable, arcing shot that lands gently inside the opponent’s kitchen, forcing them to hit the ball below the level of the net and giving you and your partner the time needed to safely sprint forward to the kitchen line.

Here is my complete mechanical breakdown for executing the drop with consistent precision.

1. Grip Pressure: The 2 to 3 Out of 10 Rule

The most prevalent mistake in pickleball is a death grip on the paddle handle. If your grip tension is an 8 or 9 out of 10, the rigid paddle face acts like a trampoline. The ball bounces off the face with unpredictable speed, sailing high into the opponent’s strike zone.

  • Hold the Paddle Like a Bird: Grip tension should be no more than a 2 or 3 on a scale of 1 to 10. You want just enough firmness to maintain control, but loose enough that the paddle absorbs the incoming kinetic energy of the ball.
  • Soft Hands Absorb Pace: A relaxed grip deadens heavy incoming returns, allowing you to control the exact distance and arc of the ball.

2. Pendulum Swing from the Shoulder (Lock the Wrist)

Tennis players transitioning to pickleball often try to "wrist flick" the drop shot, attempting to create heavy topspin. In pickleball, with a 13–16mm polymer composite paddle and a perforated plastic wiffle ball, excessive wrist movement leads to high variability and errors into the net.

  • Shoulder Pivot: The third shot drop is a gentle pendulum swing initiated from the shoulder joint. The wrist remains completely stable and locked in position throughout the stroke.
  • Open Paddle Face: At contact, the paddle face should be tilted slightly open (roughly 10–15 degrees upward).
  • Lifting Through the Ball: Rather than hitting through the ball horizontally, lift the ball from below its center with an upward push, following through smoothly toward your target at eye level.

3. Controlling the Apex: The Secret to Unattackable Drops

Many players think a good drop shot should clear the net by only half an inch. In reality, aiming too low results in 50% of your drops catching the top of the net tape.

The Apex Principle

The highest point of your ball's arc—the apex—must occur on your side of the net, roughly 2 to 3 feet in front of the net tape. If the apex occurs on your side, the ball is on a downward trajectory as it crosses the net. Even if it clears the net by a foot, gravity pulls it downward into the kitchen, ensuring your opponents cannot attack it on the fly.

4. Lower Body Preparation & Footwork

You cannot hit a delicate touch shot while off-balance or on the dead run:

  • Stop Before You Strike: Execute a balanced split-step as your opponent strikes the return. Set your feet in a balanced semi-open stance before initiating the stroke.
  • Bend from the Knees, Not the Waist: Lower your center of gravity by flexing your knees. Getting your eyes and paddle level with the ball creates clean contact without awkward scooping.
  • Forward Weight Transfer: As you lift through the stroke, gently transfer your weight onto your front foot, initiating momentum that carries you smoothly forward toward the kitchen line.

5. The Two-Step Transition to the Kitchen Line

Executing a great drop shot is only half the battle; you must capitalize on it by moving forward:

  1. Hit First, Watch Second: Complete your stroke and balance recovery before taking your first stride forward.
  2. Read the Opponent's Paddle: If you see the opponent lunging down below net level to dig out your drop, advance all the way to the kitchen line immediately.
  3. Split-Step in Transition: If your drop hangs slightly high or the opponent prepares to drive, do not keep running! Stop immediately, split-step in "no-man's land" (the transition zone), and be ready to reset the next shot softly before completing your advance.

On-Court Drill: The "10-in-a-Row" Kitchen Target Drill

Stand at the baseline with a basket of balls (or partner). Place a towel or target cone two feet inside the opponent's kitchen line.

Hit drops focusing exclusively on soft grip tension and the shoulder pendulum lift. Do not advance until you and your partner can land 7 out of 10 balls softly into the kitchen without a single ball clearing net height by more than 18 inches at crossing.
