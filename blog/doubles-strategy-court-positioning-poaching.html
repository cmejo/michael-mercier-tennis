Doubles Strategy Masterclass: Court Positioning, Poaching Tactics & Communication

By Michael Mercier • USPTA Master Professional & Former 20-Year Head Tennis Pro at Harvard University

In my 30+ years coaching tennis—including 20 years leading tennis at Harvard University and working with hundreds of adult league competitors across the North Shore of Massachusetts—I have observed a universal truth about doubles:

The team with the best groundstrokes rarely wins doubles matches. The team that understands court geometry, controls the center net, and moves as a synchronized unit wins almost every time.

Too many recreational players treat doubles like two people playing singles on the same court. They stand anchored to their positions, guard meaningless alley space, and leave the middle wide open.

Here is your tactical masterclass for elevating your doubles game, dominating USTA New England and North Shore leagues, and turning your net game into a terrifying presence.

1. Stop Guarding the Alley: The 80% Rule

The most costly mistake made by amateur net players is standing within 2 feet of the doubles alley line. When asked why, they always say: "I'm guarding my alley!"

Here is the mathematical reality:

  • The Alley Shot Is Hard: Down-the-line returns travel over the highest part of the net (3.5 feet at the posts) and have the shortest court distance (78 feet) with zero margin for error. Tour statistics and league data prove that fewer than 15% of returns ever travel down the alley, and of those, over half miss out or into the net.
  • The Middle Wins Matches: In contrast, over 75% of all doubles winners and errors occur through the middle strap of the net! When you stand by the alley, you give away the center of the court.
  • The Optimal Net Position: Take one full step closer to the center service line. Straddle the service box so you can take one diagonal forward step to cut off any ball crossing the middle strap.

2. Moving on the Diagonal (Never Lateral)

When you attempt to poach by running straight sideways (parallel to the net), your racquet strikes the ball behind your body, sending the volley wide or into the net.

The 45-Degree Poaching Rule

A true poach is a sprint forward at a 45-degree forward diagonal angle toward the center strap. Moving diagonally cuts off the ball’s trajectory earlier, allows you to strike the ball above net level, and directs the volley downward at the feet of the opposing net player.

3. The Two High-Percentage Formations Every Team Must Know

Formation #1: The Australian Formation

When facing an opponent with an exceptional cross-court return that keeps beating your server, switch to the Australian formation:

  • The net player stands on the same side of the court as the server, aligned near the center service line.
  • The server serves from close to the center hash mark and covers the open side.
  • This completely removes the receiver’s natural cross-court angle, forcing them to hit a high-risk down-the-line return over the higher part of the net.

Formation #2: The "I" Formation

The ultimate psychological weapon in modern doubles:

  • The net player crouches directly on top of the center service line (the "T"), with their chest low to the net tape.
  • The server stands near the center mark and signals behind their back whether the net player will break left or right after the serve is struck.
  • The receiver has no idea which way the net player will leap, creating hesitation, indecision, and weak returns.

4. Pre-Point Hand Signals

Eliminate on-court miscommunication with three simple behind-the-back signals from the net player:

  • Clenched Fist ("Stay"): Server covers their own half; net player protects the middle and looks for a short ball.
  • Open Hand ("Poach"): The net player is crossing the center line aggressively as soon as the server's toss goes up. The server rotates behind to cover the open court.
  • Wiggling Fingers ("Fake"): The net player feints aggressively toward the center to distract the returner, then retreats back to their side.

5. Target Selection: Where to Hit the Volley

Once you intercept the ball at the net, do not try to paint the doubles lines:

  • Primary Target: The Opposing Net Player's Feet: Hitting the ball down at the feet of the closer player gives them less than 0.3 seconds to react.
  • Secondary Target: Down the Middle Between Both Players: The middle creates hesitation, causes racquets to clash, and forces error-prone communication.
